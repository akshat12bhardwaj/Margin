# Margin secondary research evidence

The references below use the same numbers as the project report. They document affordability, reuse and design precedents in the settings studied. They provide no BMU participation, adoption, savings or environmental measurements.

## [9] Dan Xie, Student Public Interest Research Groups (2026)

- **Publication:** *Fixing the Broken Textbooks Market*, fourth edition, February 2026. [Original report](https://studentpirgs.org/assets/uploads/2026/02/Fixing-the-Broken-Textbooks-Market-Fourth-Edition.pdf)
- **Population and method:** The September–October 2025 U.S. survey retained **4,025 responses from 107 campuses across 25 states** after removing duplicates and blank submissions. The authors used anonymous forms and paper questionnaires, recruiting at campus locations and through digital outreach. This is a snapshot of respondents and does not establish a nationally representative prevalence estimate. See printed p. 12, Methodology.
- **Finding used:** **66% of surveyed students reported having skipped a textbook purchase because of cost.** The separate **70%** measure includes either a textbook or a digital access code and is not used as a textbook-only statistic. See printed pp. 2 and 7. This supports affordable discovery as a general objective. The report does not measure second-hand purchases or demand for Margin.
- **Report use:** Sections 1.1, 2.6 and 3.2.

## [10] Florida Virtual Campus (2022)

- **Publication:** Florida Virtual Campus, Office of Distance Learning & Student Services, *2022 Student Textbook and Instructional Materials Survey: Results and Findings*, September 2022. [Standalone report](https://www.flbog.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Textbook-Survey-Report.pdf) · [Full report reproduced in the Florida Board of Governors packet](https://www.flbog.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/00_March-20_2023_SteeringCommitteePacket.pdf)
- **Population and method:** An online questionnaire distributed through Florida public postsecondary institutions from March to May 2022 received **13,831 submissions**. **Thirty institutions** agreed to participate. The appendix says that the published responses include submissions naming institutions that did not formally agree to participate. See printed pp. 8–11 and Appendix A §1, p. 31.
- **Findings used:** **40.4%** selected buying used copies from the campus bookstore as a cost-saving strategy. This was a multi-select item with **4,973 selections** for that option, and the percentage is the published item result. It is not a percentage recalculated from all submissions. See Key Finding 2 and Appendix A §6, pp. 40–41. The survey also documents purchases that went unused across respondents’ academic careers, a reason to consider recirculating usable copies. See Key Finding 3, pp. 17–18, and Appendix A §8, pp. 43–44.
- **Cost context:** **32.0%** of the **11,840** eligible Spring 2022 item responses reported spending more than US$300 on textbooks that term. This is self-reported spending for an enrolled subgroup, separate from the used-book item or a price estimate for India. See Key Finding 1 and its “2021–2022 Student Textbook Costs” table.
- **Report use:** Sections 1.1, 2.6 and 3.2. Bookstore purchases show use of second-hand copies in Florida. Peer-to-peer adoption and environmental outcomes were not measured for Margin.

## [11] National Council of Applied Economic Research (2014)

- **Publication:** *The Impact of Parallel Imports of Books, Films / Music and Software on the Indian Economy with Special Reference to Students*. NCAER, January 2014. Government of India Ministry of Human Resource Development commissioned the study. [NCAER publication record](https://ncaer.org/publication/the-impact-of-parallel-imports-of-books-films-music-and-software-on-the-indian-economy-with-special-reference-to-students/) · [Full report](https://cis-india.org/a2k/blogs/ncaer-parallel-imports-report.pdf)
- **Population and method:** In October–December 2012 the team used questions to guide discussions with **418 students** at **22 institutions in five Indian cities** (Bangalore, Chennai, Delhi, Kolkata and Mumbai). Participants covered several disciplines and levels of study. See Chapter 4, §4.1 and Tables 4.1–4.3.
- **Findings used:** **373 of the 418 students** said they would check the edition when buying (p. 70, §4.4). Used books were also among the reported purchase sources (p. 67, §4.2). The source counts are multiple responses, so they do not establish a percentage of unique used-book buyers. The study provides historical Indian context for displaying edition information and facilitating comparison. Its age and sample limit inference about present-day BMU.
- **Report use:** Sections 1.1, 2.6 and 3.2.

## [12] Bit-Chan Kim and Sang-Yong Lee (2018)

- **Publication:** “Used Textbook Trading Platform to Recommend University Textbooks.” *Journal of Digital Convergence*, 16(4), 329–334. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.14400/JDC.2018.16.4.329) · [Korean Citation Index record and original abstract](https://www.kci.go.kr/kciportal/landing/article.kci?arti_id=ART002338186)
- **Scope and method:** The accessible abstract describes the development of BookCue, a used-textbook platform for university students, by authors at Kongju National University in South Korea. The abstract does not establish its survey sample size, a rollout population or a measured evaluation of platform outcomes. The report uses only the author-described design.
- **Design described:** BookCue combines recommendations for textbooks and reference books with consignment trading. It is a precedent for a textbook-specific platform. Margin uses searchable listings and direct buyer–seller contact. Recommendations and consignment were not adopted from BookCue.
- **Report use:** Section 2.6 only. No survey numbers or savings claims from the abstract were included.

## Other leads screened

- The [Student PIRGs research index](https://studentpirgs.org/our-textbooks-research/) leads to the original fourth edition. The index was not counted as independent evidence.
- Barnes, Vine and Nadeau’s [2024 peer-reviewed article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.acalib.2024.102864) compares student and faculty survey waves at Franklin & Marshall College in Pennsylvania. Its abstract reports lower overall spending alongside some students going without more required materials due to cost. The publisher’s full text could not be verified in this review. It was omitted from the report to avoid relying on unverified sample details and to keep the evidence proportional.
- A [2025 Andhra Pradesh paper](https://www.ijrls.in/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/ijrls-1857.pdf) studies library-led textbook affordability. Its abstract identifies 400 students and 100 faculty, while the cost results label 500 respondents as students. Its quantitative estimates were excluded because the denominators do not reconcile clearly.
- [Northwestern’s student financial wellness guidance](https://www.northwestern.edu/financial-wellness/saving-in-college/buying-books.html) describes students using a peer textbook exchange and recommends searching by ISBN. It is a useful campus example, with no adoption or impact figures used here.
